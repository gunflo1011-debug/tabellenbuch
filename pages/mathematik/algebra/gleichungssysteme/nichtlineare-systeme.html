Nonlinear systems

A system is nonlinear when at least one equation is nonlinear in its unknowns. Solutions are the points that satisfy every equation simultaneously.

MeaningMethodsWorked exampleNumerical methodsCommon mistakesPractice

1. What is a nonlinear system?

Examples include systems containing powers such as \(x^2\), products such as \(xy\), roots, exponentials or trigonometric terms. Geometrically, solving a system means finding the common intersection points of its equations.

\[\begin{cases}x+y=5\\x^2+y^2=13\end{cases}\]

The first equation is linear, while the second describes a circle, so the system as a whole is nonlinear. Depending on the equations, a nonlinear system can have no solution, one solution, several isolated solutions, or infinitely many solutions.

2. Main solving methods

SubstitutionIsolate a variable in one equation and substitute it into another. This is often the cleanest method for small systems.
EliminationCombine equations to remove a variable or a repeated nonlinear term, then solve the reduced equation.
Numerical iterationWhen algebraic reduction is impractical, approximate a solution with a method such as Newton's method and verify the residual.

3. Worked example: line and circle

Solve

\[\begin{cases}x+y=5\\x^2+y^2=13.\end{cases}\]

From the first equation, \(y=5-x\). Substitute into the second:

\[x^2+(5-x)^2=13\\2x^2-10x+12=0\\x^2-5x+6=0\\(x-2)(x-3)=0.\]

Thus \(x=2\) or \(x=3\). Back-substitution gives the two solutions \((2,3)\) and \((3,2)\). Checking either pair in both original equations confirms it exactly.

4. When numerical methods are useful

For a vector equation \(F(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}\), Newton's method linearizes the system near the current guess \(\mathbf{x}_k\). If the Jacobian \(J_F(\mathbf{x}_k)\) is invertible, solve

\[J_F(\mathbf{x}_k)\,\Delta\mathbf{x}=-F(\mathbf{x}_k),\qquad \mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathbf{x}_k+\Delta\mathbf{x}.\]

This is a local method: convergence depends on the starting point and the behavior of the Jacobian. A numerical answer should therefore be checked by evaluating the residuals in the original equations.

5. Common mistakes

  • Stopping after substitution without back-substituting into every original equation.
  • Forgetting domain restrictions introduced by denominators, roots or logarithms.
  • Treating squaring as an equivalence transformation; it can introduce extraneous solutions.
  • Assuming a numerical solver found every solution rather than one solution near its starting guess.
